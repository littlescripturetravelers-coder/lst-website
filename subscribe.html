✦ Why a story, and why the same four travelers

A principle explained to a four-year-old tends to slide off. The same principle lived by a character they know sticks, because the child watches someone their own size make the choice and feel what comes of it.

That is why the cast never changes. Beau, Scout, Willow, and Wren show up every single night, so your child is catching up with friends at bedtime instead of meeting strangers. The routine does the heavy lifting, and the story does the teaching, so you can simply read and be present.

There is a measurable side to this too. Researchers at Ohio State estimated that reading one picture book a day gives a child about 78,000 words of exposure a year, and that children from literacy-rich homes hear roughly 1.4 million more words before kindergarten than children who are never read to. (Logan et al., Ohio State University)

✦ Why the same song every night

A lullaby is a signal, and it does more work than background music. Sung at the same point in the same order every night, it becomes something the body learns to answer before a single word of instruction is spoken, which is why bedtime resistance so often fades once the song is fixed.

Nothing to negotiate, nothing to announce. The song starts, and the room changes.

This has been measured. In a study of 144 infants published in Nature Human Behaviour, babies relaxed measurably, with slower heart rates and reduced arousal, when they heard lullabies, including lullabies from unfamiliar cultures in languages they had never heard. The calming appears to be built into what a lullaby is. (Bainbridge et al., Nature Human Behaviour)

The Virtue Library
★ 91 Stories, 36 Virtues ★ Five Ways to Search ★ 3 Stories Free

Most of us reach for the same three or four stories we happen to remember. This is the catalogue behind the whole series: every story tagged by what it teaches, so you can look up the thing your child is actually facing and walk into the room already holding the right one.

✦ Five ways to look something up
  • ✦By virtue or principle: thirty-six of them, from Courage and Obedience to Keeping Promises and Repentance.
  • ✦By life situation: the twenty moments that actually happen: a lie, a lost temper, a first day, a hard goodbye.
  • ✦By feeling: start from what your child is feeling tonight rather than from what you want to teach.
  • ✦By Traveler: every story Beau, Scout, Willow, or Wren carries, for the child who has picked a favorite.
  • ✦By scripture: go the other direction and find the story that belongs to the verse you are already reading.

✦ Why it is organized this way

Knowing is only the beginning. No catalogue can tell you what your child's heart needs tonight. This is a tool, and you are the one who knows them. Ask Heavenly Father, and then use what you find here to teach with.

Browsing is open to everyone, and three complete stories are free, with no card and no subscription. The Lantern opens all ninety-one.

Researchers testing whether storybooks actually shift children's moral beliefs found that most did not. Abstract fables about talking animals changed very little. Stories worked when they were directly applicable to the situation the child was already dealing with. That is the entire reason this library is organized by the problem in front of you tonight instead of by book order. (Psychology Today, on children's moral learning from storybooks)

✦ Why the three questions come at 5 AM

The three questions in A Parent's Heart are written for you, the parent, and were never meant as a quiz for your child. They arrive early on purpose, so they can sit with you through the whole day.

  • 1.A moment from your own life when you lived what tonight's story teaches.
  • 2.What that principle actually feels like on a hard day, something your child can recognize in you.
  • 3.A grandparent or ancestor who lived it before you were born, and how their example still reaches your family.

You read them over breakfast and let them turn over quietly while you work, drive, and fold laundry. By the time the lights go low, you are doing more than reading a story to your child. You are telling them about the time your grandmother held on, and the scripture story has become your family's story too.

This is the part parents tell us they did not expect. Researchers at Emory University's Family Narratives Lab found that children in families who told coherent, emotionally open stories about hard family moments had better self-esteem, stronger social competence, higher-quality friendships, and less anxiety and stress. (Fivush & Duke, Emory University)
