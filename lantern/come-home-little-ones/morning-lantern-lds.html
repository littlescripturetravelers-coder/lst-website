📖 Tonight's Scripture Story
Come Home, Little Ones
Heavenly Father gave Jeremiah words to call out toward the north. His children had wandered a long way from home.
‘Come back,’ the words said. ‘I am merciful. I will not keep my anger forever.’
He promised to gather them, one from a city and two from a family. He would carry them home again to Zion.
And He would give them shepherds after His own heart. They would feed His children with knowing and with understanding.
Heavenly Father counts His children one by one, and He knows exactly where you are tonight. If you ever wander, the door stays open. ‘Come home’ is His softest word.
★ Scripture Traveler of the Night
Willow the Warm
"Warm hugs, warm hearts!"
Willow fits tonight because mercy is the tenderness that leaves the door standing open. In Willow’s world today: Willow’s little brother knocked her block tower flat and ran to hide behind the couch. Willow looked at the mess for a long, quiet minute. Then she called out softly, ‘You can come out. We can build it again.’ Just like Heavenly Father calling His children home, Willow learned that love does not stay angry forever.
★ The Heart of Tonight's Story
Gospel Principle: Mercy calls first. Israel had walked away from Heavenly Father, and the northern tribes had been gone for a hundred years. He still told Jeremiah to call toward the north. Return, He said. I am merciful. I will not keep anger for ever. Nobody had come back yet when He said it. The call comes before the turning. That is how He treats you, too.
Virtue: Compassion & Tenderness, because mercy is the tenderness that leaves the door standing open. Tonight’s feeling word is homesick. It is a grown-up word as much as a small one. Most of us know the ache of being far from where we belong. Jeremiah names the ache, and then he names the way back. It is one step long, and it is called Return.
Applying It to All Children and Adolescent Development Stages
Early Years (Ages 3–5): Open your arms wide and say, Come home. Let them run into the hug. Tell them Heavenly Father’s arms are open like this, even after a bad day.
Early Elementary (Ages 6–8): Read verse 12 together and find the two promises: I am merciful, and I will not stay angry. Ask what Willow said to her brother behind the couch, and practice saying it.
Late Elementary (Ages 9–11): Talk about what it feels like to hide after doing wrong. Show them that Heavenly Father calls first, before we fix anything. Ask who at school might need a come-home voice.
Early Adolescence (Ages 12–14): They may believe one mistake has shut a door for good. Tell them about a time you came back and were welcomed. Say plainly that repentance is a door, not a wall.
Teen (Ages 15–17): Be honest about anger, yours and theirs. Ask how long anger lasts in your house, and whether it should. Read verse 15 and ask what a shepherd after God’s own heart does with a wanderer.
Young Adult (Ages 18+): Text them the verse with no lecture attached. Let them know the door at home stays open whatever they decide this year. Then ask whom they could call home.
✦ Verse of the Day
"Go and proclaim these words toward the north, and say, Return, thou backsliding Israel, saith the LORD; and I will not cause mine anger to fall upon you: for I am merciful, saith the LORD, and I will not keep anger for ever."
Jeremiah 3:12 (King James Version)
Simple truth: Read the verse as three promises. He speaks toward the north, where the lost ones went, so the call goes where they are. He will not let His anger fall. And He will not keep it for ever. The word merciful is the hinge. Everything He asks, which is only Return, rests on what He is. He is kind even when you have done wrong. You can come back to Someone like that.

Jeremiah 3:14–15

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Today, think of one person you have been angry with for too long. A spouse. A parent. A grown son or daughter who stopped calling. Jeremiah’s God does not keep anger for ever, and He asks the same of us. You do not need a speech. Willow used nine words: You can come out. We can build it again. Find your own nine words, and say them before the day ends. Mercy is the first move, not the reward.

Why This Story Matters
Everybody wanders. Not always far, and not always on purpose. A habit slides, a prayer gets skipped, a hard word hardens into a wall. Jeremiah 3 was written for people who had been gone a long time. Heavenly Father’s answer was not a search party with torches. It was a voice calling home. For a tired parent that is good news. The way back is shorter than the way out.
Willow’s story is the same lesson at living-room size. Her brother flattens her tower and hides behind the couch. She looks at the mess for a long, quiet minute. That minute is the whole story. She could stay angry. She calls out softly instead. You can come out. We can build it again. Notice she offers to rebuild, not to pretend. Mercy does not deny the mess. It just refuses to end the relationship over it.
You will be on both sides of this verse in one week. Some days you are the one who wandered. Some days you are the one at the door. Jeremiah tells you what Heavenly Father does in both cases. He calls, He waits, and He keeps the light on. The Remember line says it the way a small voice can say it. He will not stay angry forever.
Historical Background
Jeremiah began to preach around 627 BC, in the days of young King Josiah. Judah still had its temple and its king. The northern kingdom, Israel, did not. Assyria had carried those ten tribes away about a hundred years earlier, and nobody had heard from them since. When Heavenly Father says to call toward the north, He means those lost tribes. He had not forgotten where they went.
Chapters 2 and 3 use the picture of a marriage. Israel is the wife who walked out, and the Lord is the husband who was wronged. Under the law of Moses a divorced wife who married again could not simply come back. Jeremiah 3 sets that rule aside on purpose. The Lord says, I am married unto you, and Return. The people who heard it knew exactly how surprising that was.
Zion in verse 14 is Jerusalem, the city with the temple, the place where Israel met with God. Pastors in verse 15 is an old English word for shepherds. In Jeremiah’s world kings and priests were called shepherds, and most of them had failed the flock. The Lord promises new shepherds, chosen by His own heart, who feed people with knowledge. Josiah’s reform was beginning around this time. For a short while, it looked possible.

Word Study — Jeremiah 3:12, 14
Shuv (שׁוּב) Chasid (חָסִיד) Natar (נָטַר)
Shuv — “return” (vv. 12, 14). The most common word for turning around in the Old Testament. It is the word for repentance, and it is also the root of the word for backsliding, because both are turnings. Jeremiah uses it both ways in these verses. Turn back, you who turned away. Tonight it matters because repentance is not a long program. It is one turn, toward home.
Chasid — “merciful” (v. 12). The word comes from chesed, the steady, loyal love of a covenant, a two-way promise. It is the love that stays after the other side has broken its word. When the Lord says I am merciful, He is naming His character, not His mood. That is why the door stays open. It is held open by who He is.
Natar — “keep” anger (v. 12). The word means to guard or to hold onto something, the way you keep a grudge. The Lord says He will not do that for ever. Many of us do. Anger kept past its time turns into a wall between two people. Tonight the Lord shows the other way. He sets it down.
★ Where Jeremiah Says It Again
The same open door, four more times, across the book
Jeremiah 3:22 · 24 · 29 · 31
Jeremiah 3:12 is not the only place Jeremiah shows the Lord calling His people home. Ten verses later, in 3:22, the call comes again, and this time He promises to heal the very thing that made them wander. In chapter 24 He promises to bring the exiles back and give them a heart to know Him. In chapter 29 He tells people far away in Babylon that when they seek Him with their whole heart, He will be found, and He will gather them. In chapter 31 He says He has loved them with an everlasting love, and that He hears Ephraim grieving in the north and will surely have mercy.
Read the four together and one thing stands out. Every time, the people are far away and the Lord is the one who speaks first. Nobody earns the call. Jeremiah had the hardest words of any prophet for his own nation, and he never took them back. But when he describes what the Lord is like, he keeps reaching for the same picture: a door left open and a voice calling through it. That is the picture to carry into your own day.
The Line to Carry Out of Tonight
"Heavenly Father will not stay angry forever, and He keeps a light burning for every single one of us!"
— Remember This, from tonight's story in Little Scripture Travelers
Say the two halves slowly. He will not stay angry. He keeps a light burning. The first half is about Him. The second half is about you, and about whoever you have been waiting on. Most of us know what it is to stand on a porch and wonder if someone will come home. Jeremiah says Heavenly Father stands there with the light on, for every single one. Including the one who went the farthest. Including you.
💬 Conversation Starter
"What did Heavenly Father ask His children to do?"
"Who did He promise to send to feed and care for them?"
"What did Willow say to her brother hiding behind the couch?"
Then turn it toward them: "When have you felt far from someone you love and wanted to go back?"
💬 Go-Deeper Conversation
"Jeremiah’s God calls first and waits for the turning. When someone in my life wronged me, who moved first, and what did it cost me to wait?"
"I am merciful is the reason He gives for the call. When I set a boundary with someone I love, do they still hear mercy in it, or only the wall?"
"Willow looked at the mess for a long, quiet minute before she spoke. What would change in my house if I took that minute before I answered?"
✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Start with your own north. Everyone has a direction they drift in when they are tired: a screen, a silence, a sharp tongue. Name yours out loud to Heavenly Father tonight. Then hear verse 12 as a sentence spoken to you. Return. I am merciful. The turning is small. It is one honest sentence in prayer, and the walk home starts there.
Then do the thing the Five Stones ask, and name the feeling. The word is homesick. Grown-ups get it too, for a person, a place, or a version of themselves they lost. Say the word. A feeling with a name can be carried to the Lord. A feeling without a name just sits in the chest and aches.
And set one piece of anger down before the day ends. The Lord says He will not keep anger for ever, and He asks you to be like Him. Pick the oldest grudge you are carrying and decide what you will do with it this week. A phone call. A note. A prayer for the person by name. You do not have to feel ready. Willow did not feel ready. She called out anyway.

In tonight’s Little Scripture Travelers story, Willow the Warm stands exactly where Jeremiah stood. Someone she loves has done wrong and run off to hide. Tonight’s Five Stones do the naming for you. SPOT the people far away, and Heavenly Father wanting them back. NAME the feeling: homesick. FIND the moment: When have you felt far from someone you love and wanted to go back? Then the FORK, which is the whole lesson. Stay angry forever, or keep calling them home. Heavenly Father kept calling. Ask the FIND question exactly as it is written, and then wait for the answer.
Do the Feeling Moment with your whole body, because this one was built for arms. Cup your hands around your mouth and call very softly across the room: Come home. Then open both arms as wide as they will go for a welcoming hug. Then the CALM stone: arms wide, a hug around yourself, and a whisper, Heavenly Father, I am coming home. Let that be the last thing said before the light goes off.

The Full Passage in Context
What comes before. Chapter 3 opens with a hard question. If a man puts away his wife and she marries another, can she come back to him? Under the law, no. Then the Lord lays out how far Israel wandered, and how Judah watched and learned nothing. Verse 11 says the sister who left first was less guilty than the sister who stayed and pretended. That is the setup for the softest call in the book.
How the passage builds. Verse 12 is the call toward the north: Return, I am merciful, I will not keep anger for ever. Verse 13 asks for one thing, honesty: only acknowledge what you did. Verse 14 gives the picture of the gathering, one from a city and two from a family, and brings them to Zion. Verse 15 promises shepherds after the Lord’s own heart, who feed with knowledge and understanding. Call, confession, gathering, shepherds. The order matters.
What comes after. Verses 16 to 18 look far ahead. Jerusalem itself will be called the throne of the Lord, and Judah and Israel will walk home together out of the north. Verses 19 to 25 are a conversation. The Lord longs to be called Father. The people finally answer, in verse 22, and come back. Chapter 4 opens with the same word one more time: if you will return, return to Me.
Second Word Study — Jeremiah 3:14–15
Ba’al (בָּעַל) Ro’im (רֹעִים) Kelibbi (כְּלִבִּי)
Ba’al — “married” (v. 14). The verb means to be bound to someone as a husband. Israel had been running after false gods that carried the same name, Baal. The Lord takes the word back for Himself. I am the one who is bound to you, He says, not those idols. For you it means the promise did not break when you did. He is still bound.
Ro’im — “pastors” (v. 15). Simply shepherds, the people who feed and guard a flock. Jeremiah uses the word for kings, priests and prophets, and most of them in his book fail at it. The promise here is shepherds chosen by the Lord’s own heart. Tonight it matters because you are a shepherd to someone. The verse tells you what kind to be.
Kelibbi — “according to mine heart” (v. 15). Shepherds shaped like His heart. Not the loudest or the most impressive, but the ones who feed with knowledge and understanding. In Hebrew the heart is where you think and decide, not only where you feel. A shepherd after His heart decides the way He decides. He calls first. He does not keep anger.

Commentary — What Has Been Taught
Readers of Jeremiah 3 have long noticed how the chapter argues with itself. The law said the wife who left and remarried could not come back. The Lord says Return anyway. Most teachers read this as the point, not a problem. Mercy is bigger than the rule that made the mercy necessary. Jeremiah is not saying the law was wrong. He is saying the Lord’s heart is larger than the case the law described.
Latter-day Saint teaching reads verses 14 and 15 with the gathering of Israel in view. The Lord takes one from a city and two from a family. That is how the Church has watched the gathering happen for two hundred years. Not whole nations at once, but one person and one household at a time. Living prophets have called that gathering the most important work on the earth today. Shepherds after His own heart is read beside the promise of living prophets and local leaders who feed the flock with true knowledge.
Where faithful readers differ. Some read the call to the north as fulfilled when exiles came home in Ezra’s day. Others hear it as still unfolding, as scattered Israel is gathered in the last days. Many hold both. For a parent, the two readings land in the same place. The Lord calls His wanderers back, in Jeremiah’s day and in yours, one at a time.
A Doctrinal Thread — Mercy That Calls Us Home
Exodus 34:6–7. The Lord passes before Moses and names Himself: merciful and gracious, longsuffering, abundant in goodness. The first thing Israel ever learned about His character.
Psalm 103:8–9. David sings it. Slow to anger, plenteous in mercy, and He will not keep His anger for ever. Jeremiah borrows the line.
Jeremiah 3:12. Tonight. The call toward the north, the mercy named, the anger that will not be kept.
Luke 15:20. The father sees the son a great way off, has compassion, and runs. The Savior telling Jeremiah’s story with a face on it.
Alma 5:33. The arms of mercy are extended toward all, and the invitation goes to everyone. Come unto Me.

"Heavenly Father will not stay angry forever, and He keeps a light burning for every single one of us!"
— Remember This, from tonight's story in Little Scripture Travelers
Say the two halves slowly. He will not stay angry. He keeps a light burning. The first half is about Him. The second half is about you, and about whoever you have been waiting on. Most of us know what it is to stand on a porch and wonder if someone will come home. Jeremiah says Heavenly Father stands there with the light on, for every single one. Including the one who went the farthest. Including you.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Start with your own north. Everyone has a direction they drift in when they are tired: a screen, a silence, a sharp tongue. Name yours out loud to Heavenly Father tonight. Then hear verse 12 as a sentence spoken to you. Return. I am merciful. The turning is small. It is one honest sentence in prayer, and the walk home starts there.
Then do the thing the Five Stones ask, and name the feeling. The word is homesick. Grown-ups get it too, for a person, a place, or a version of themselves they lost. Say the word. A feeling with a name can be carried to the Lord. A feeling without a name just sits in the chest and aches.
And set one piece of anger down before the day ends. The Lord says He will not keep anger for ever, and He asks you to be like Him. Pick the oldest grudge you are carrying and decide what you will do with it this week. A phone call. A note. A prayer for the person by name. You do not have to feel ready. Willow did not feel ready. She called out anyway.

✦ Connecting the Dots
Where this doctrine begins, how it grows, and how it reaches your own front door
The idea in tonight’s chapter starts on Mount Sinai. Moses asks to see the Lord, and the Lord passes by and names Himself: merciful and gracious, longsuffering, abundant in goodness. That is the first thing Israel ever learned about His character. Jeremiah leans on it when he says, I am merciful. He is quoting the oldest line in the family.
David turns it into a song. Psalm 103 says the Lord is slow to anger, will not always chide, and will not keep His anger for ever. Jeremiah 3:12 uses the same words a few hundred years later, as a call to people who had left. The song became an invitation.
Then the Savior told the story Himself. A son takes his inheritance, leaves for a far country, and wastes it. When he turns for home, the father sees him a great way off and runs. That running father is Jeremiah’s voice toward the north, with a face on it. The call did not wait for the son to arrive. It went out to meet him.
The Restoration adds the part Jeremiah could only promise. Alma says the arms of mercy are extended toward all, and the invitation goes to everyone. The Doctrine and Covenants says that when a person repents, the Lord remembers the sin no more. Moroni records that as often as people repented with real intent, they were forgiven. The door Jeremiah saw is on its hinges still.
So it arrives at your own kitchen table in a form small enough to use today. You will wander this week, and someone you love will wander from you. The Lord calls first, waits, and keeps the light burning. Be the voice toward the north. Then go and be the one who comes home.

✦ Three Passages to Sit With
Longer readings matched to tonight's text
Reading one · ten minutes
Jeremiah 3, read straight through
Read the whole chapter once, out loud if you can. It opens with the hard question about a wife who left, and it ends with the people answering the call and coming home. In between, the Lord names the wandering plainly and then says Return anyway. Tonight’s story is verses 12 to 15. Reading the chapter whole shows you why those verses land so softly: everything before them is the mess, and everything after them is the welcome.
Reading two · fifteen minutes
Luke 15
Three lost things in one chapter: a sheep, a coin, and a son. Read all three and notice who does the searching in each one. The shepherd goes out. The woman lights a lamp. The father runs down the road. Jeremiah 3:12 is the Old Testament version of that running father. Read the son’s speech in verses 18 and 19, and then notice that the father never lets him finish it.
Reading three · ten minutes
3 Nephi 9:13–22 and Alma 5:33–36
In 3 Nephi 9 the risen Lord speaks out of the darkness to a people who have just lived through the destruction. The first thing He asks them is whether they will return to Him now, and He says His arm of mercy is extended. In Alma 5 the invitation is the same, given to people who still have a choice to make. Read them back to back and count how many times the Lord says come. He never stops saying it.
✦ Study Tactics — Going to Another Level
Four Latter-day Saint study methods, sized for tonight
1 · Scripture journaling — twenty minutes, one page. Copy Jeremiah 3:12 by hand. Underline the three things the Lord says about Himself: I am merciful, I will not cause anger to fall, I will not keep anger for ever. Beside each one write the name of a person who needs that from you this week. Then write the name of the person who gave it to you.
2 · Footnote and Topical Guide chains — fifteen minutes. Open Jeremiah 3:12 in your own scriptures and follow the footnotes. Then look up Mercy and Repent in the Topical Guide. Read a dozen entries from Exodus to the Doctrine and Covenants and count how often the Lord is the one who speaks first.
3 · Liken the scriptures — ten minutes, out loud. Read verses 12 to 15 aloud and put your own name where it says Israel. Then read it again with the name of someone you have been waiting on. Nephi said to liken all scripture to ourselves. This chapter was written to people who had been gone a long time, and most of us have been there.
4 · Pondering and recording impressions — five minutes, tonight. Ask one question: where is the Lord calling me back right now, that I have been calling too far gone? Write the first thing that comes, without fixing the words. Verse 12 says Return. Recording is how you notice the voice.
✦ One Invitation This Week
Call first, all week. Every time someone in your house does wrong and pulls away, your first move is a voice, not a verdict. Count ten slow breaths, then call them back the way Willow did. Tell the family this is the week’s rule and let them catch you breaking it. Jeremiah’s God calls toward the north, and so will your house.
Then, on the seventh night, ask the FIND question from tonight’s Feeling Moment one more time. When have you felt far from someone you love and wanted to go back? Listen to every answer. Then tell your own. A family that can say I wandered, and I was called home has learned Jeremiah 3.
