📖 Tonight's Scripture Story
My Hope Since I Was Little
One song-writer had grown old, and he looked back on his whole long life. He remembered that Heavenly Father had been with him the entire time.
“Heavenly Father, You have been my hope since I was very young,” he sang. “You have taken care of me all my life.”
From the time he was a tiny child until his hair turned gray, Heavenly Father had never once left his side. Heavenly Father has been taking care of you since before you were even born — and He always will. He is with us when we’re little, when we’re big, and every day in between. His love never stops.
★ Scripture Traveler of the Night
Willow the Warm
"Warm hugs, warm hearts!"
Willow fits tonight because she feels Heavenly Father’s gentle, lifelong love. In Willow’s world today: Willow looked at baby pictures and giggled at how tiny she used to be. Mom said, “Heavenly Father has loved you and watched over you this whole time.” Willow’s heart felt warm and safe, knowing she was never once alone. Just like the psalm-writer trusted Heavenly Father his whole life, Willow felt His love from the very beginning until now.
★ The Heart of Tonight's Story
Gospel Principle: Heavenly Father’s care has no gaps. Psalm 71 is sung by someone who has grown old. He looks back over his whole life and sees one thing everywhere. The Lord was there. From before birth, through youth, into old age. He was never once absent. That long view gives the old man hope for whatever is left. It can give a child hope for everything ahead.
Virtue: Hope & Lifelong Trust, because hope grows from remembering. Tonight’s feeling word is secure. The book calls it the watched-over, safe-all-along feeling. A child who knows they have always been cared for feels secure. A child who knows they always will be has hope.
For young children: When you were a tiny baby, Heavenly Father was taking care of you. Now that you are bigger, He still is. When you grow all the way up, He will be there too. His love never stops.
✦ Verse of the Day
"For thou art my hope, O Lord GOD: thou art my trust from my youth."
Psalm 71:5 (King James Version)
Simple truth: Two words carry this verse: hope and trust. Hope looks forward. Trust leans its weight on something. The writer has done both since he was young. Now he is old, and he can say the Lord never let him down. Read verses 17 and 18 too. You have taught me since my youth. Now that I am old and gray, do not leave me. It is one life, held from start to finish.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Tonight or tomorrow, do what Willow’s mom did. Pull out a few baby pictures, or scroll back on your phone. Let your child giggle at how tiny they were. Then say it plainly. Heavenly Father has loved you and watched over you this whole time. Point to each picture. He was there. He was there. He was there. A child who sees their own life as one long story of being cared for learns hope without a lesson.

Why This Story Matters
Children live in the now. Yesterday feels far away and next year is unimaginable. That makes every hard day feel like it will last forever. Psalm 71 gives them a longer view. Heavenly Father was with you before you could remember, and He will be with you after today. A child with that view is steadier.
Willow’s story shows it in a family room. She looks at baby pictures and giggles at how tiny she was. Mom tells her Heavenly Father has loved her the whole time. Willow’s heart feels warm and safe, knowing she was never once alone. The book says she felt His love from the very beginning until now. That is the psalm, sized for a five-year-old.
For parents, this psalm is a gift for the middle of life. You are somewhere between youth and gray hair. It is easy to see only this year’s worries. Psalm 71 asks you to look back and count the times He carried you. Then it asks you to tell the next generation. That is what tonight is for.
Historical Background
Psalm 71 has no title, but many readers think it was written by David in old age. First Kings describes King David at the end of his life, old and cold, unable to get warm. Whoever wrote it, the singer is clearly old, with gray hair, looking back over a long life with God.
Something lovely is hidden in the psalm. Many of its lines are borrowed from other psalms, especially Psalms 22, 31 and 40. The opening verses are almost the same as Psalm 31. It sounds like an old man praying with the songs he learned when he was young. Those songs had become his own words.
In Israel, the old were expected to pass on the story. Psalm 78, in this same week’s reading, says it plainly. We will not hide these things from our children, but tell the next generation the praises of the Lord. Psalm 71 says the same. Until I have shown your strength to this generation. An old man’s last job was to tell the young.

Word Study — Psalm 71:5
Tiqvah (תִּקְוָה) Mivtach (מִבְטָח) Neurim (נְעוּרִים)
Tiqvah — “hope” (v. 5). The same word also means a cord or rope. When Rahab hung a scarlet cord from her window to save her family, the Bible uses this word. Hope, in Hebrew, is something you can hold on to. The Lord is that cord from youth to old age.
Mivtach — “trust,” or confidence (v. 5). It means a place where you can lean your weight and not fall. Not a wish, but something solid. The old man says the Lord has been his leaning place since he was young. That is a good thing to tell a child who is still learning to lean.
Ne’urim — “youth” (v. 5, 17). The years of being young, from childhood into early adulthood. The psalm goes back further still, to the womb in verse 6. Your child is in their ne’urim right now. This is the season the old man looked back on and called the beginning of his trust.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Start with the pictures. Psalm 71 is a look back over a whole life. Make that visible for your child. Lay out a few photos in order, from baby to now. Tell one short story for each. Here you were sick, and Heavenly Father helped you get better. Here we moved, and He found us good friends. Let your child see their life as one story with Him in every chapter.
Then do the same for yourself. Answer the first Parent’s Heart question honestly. Where do you see Heavenly Father’s hand the whole way? Write down five moments in your life when He carried you. Share one with your child this week. The psalm says to tell the next generation. You are the one who can.
And invite a grandparent in. The third Parent’s Heart question asks about an ancestor who trusted Heavenly Father all their life. If a grandparent is living, ask them to tell your child how He helped them. If not, tell what you know. An old person’s testimony is exactly what Psalm 71 is.

The Full Passage in Context
Verses 1 to 8: from the womb. In you, Lord, I have taken refuge. Be my rock, my strong house, where I can always go. You have been my hope since my youth. I have leaned on you since birth. I have become a sign to many, the singer says, but you are my strong refuge. My mouth is filled with your praise all day long.
Verses 9 to 18: into old age. Do not cast me off when I am old. Do not leave me when my strength is gone. Enemies say God has left him, but he keeps hoping and praising. Verses 17 and 18 hold the whole psalm. You have taught me since my youth. Even when I am old and gray, do not leave me, until I tell the next generation.
Verses 19 to 24: still singing. Your righteousness reaches the heavens. You have shown me hard troubles, but you will bring me up again. You will comfort me on every side. I will praise you with the harp and sing with the lyre. The old man ends with music, like a child who never stopped singing.
Second Word Study — Psalm 71:3, 18
Maon (מָעוֹן) Sevah (שֵׂיבָה) Dor (דּוֹר)
Ma’on — “habitation,” a dwelling place (v. 3). Not just a shelter in a storm, but a home you live in. The singer asks the Lord to be a house he can always come back to. That is what Heavenly Father is for a child. Not just help in emergencies, but home.
Sevah — “gray hair,” or old age (v. 18). The same word Isaiah uses when the Lord promises to carry His people to gray hairs. In Israel gray hair was honored. Proverbs calls it a crown of glory. The book says from the time he was a tiny child until his hair turned gray. That is this word.
Dor — “generation” (v. 18). The people of one age, passing the story to the next. The old man’s last request is to have time to tell the young about the Lord’s strength. Tonight you are doing exactly that. You are one dor telling the next.

Commentary — What Has Been Taught
Readers have long called Psalm 71 the prayer of an old saint. Many have noticed how it weaves together lines from earlier psalms. It sounds like a man who prayed the Psalms all his life, until their words became his own. That is a picture of what a lifetime of scripture does.
Latter-day Saint teaching gives this psalm a longer view still. We lived with Heavenly Father before we were born. The Lord told Jeremiah, before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee, and Abraham saw the spirits before the world was. So when the book says He has been taking care of you since before you were born, Latter-day Saints hear that literally.
Where faithful readers differ. Some read Psalm 71 as David’s own prayer in his last years. Others think it was written later, by an unnamed believer, using David’s words. The psalm does not tell us. For a child, it does not matter. An old person trusted Heavenly Father their whole life, and so can they.

Start with the pictures. Psalm 71 is a look back over a whole life. Make that visible for your child. Lay out a few photos in order, from baby to now. Tell one short story for each. Here you were sick, and Heavenly Father helped you get better. Here we moved, and He found us good friends. Let your child see their life as one story with Him in every chapter.
Then do the same for yourself. Answer the first Parent’s Heart question honestly. Where do you see Heavenly Father’s hand the whole way? Write down five moments in your life when He carried you. Share one with your child this week. The psalm says to tell the next generation. You are the one who can.
And invite a grandparent in. The third Parent’s Heart question asks about an ancestor who trusted Heavenly Father all their life. If a grandparent is living, ask them to tell your child how He helped them. If not, tell what you know. An old person’s testimony is exactly what Psalm 71 is.

✦ Connecting the Dots
Where this truth begins, how it grows, and how it reaches your child
It begins with Jacob, old and near death, blessing his grandsons. He calls the Lord the God who has shepherded him all his life. The first long look back in scripture, and it sees care everywhere.
Samuel shows the other end. A small boy at the tabernacle, and the Lord with him as he grew. Psalm 71 joins the two ends together. Trust from youth, still trusting with gray hair. One life, held all the way.
Isaiah turns it into a promise for everyone. I have carried you from birth, and I will carry you to old age. Psalm 139 adds that He knew us before birth, and wrote our days in His book.
Then the Restoration opens the view even wider. We lived with Heavenly Father before we came here. Abraham saw the spirits before the world, and the Doctrine and Covenants speaks of lessons learned before birth. His care began before memory, and it will not end at death.
So it reaches your child at a baby picture tonight. You were tiny, and He was there. You are bigger, and He is here. You will grow tall, and He will be there too.
✦ Ten Places Scripture Says It
Held from start to finish, traced across the standard works
Ten references, described in our own words rather than quoted. Open them yourself; the descriptions are ours, the words are not.
Genesis 48:15 — My shepherd all my life long.
1 Samuel 3:19 — Samuel grew, and the Lord was with him.
Psalm 22:9–10 — You made me trust you at my mother’s breast.
Psalm 71:5 — Tonight. My hope, my trust from my youth.
Psalm 71:17–18 — Now that I am old and gray, do not forsake me.
Psalm 139:16 — All my days were written in your book.
Isaiah 46:4 — Even to gray hairs I will carry you.
Mosiah 2:21 — Preserving you from day to day.
Mormon 1:2 — A sober child, quick to observe.
Doctrine and Covenants 138:56 — First lessons in the world of spirits.
Read them in order and they cover a whole life. Before birth, childhood, youth, old age. There is no chapter where He is missing.

Longer readings matched to tonight's text
Reading one · ten minutes
Psalm 71, all twenty-four verses
Read it as an old person’s prayer. Mark every word about time: youth, birth, old age, gray hair, all day long, generation. Notice how the psalm moves from fear of being left to praise with the harp.
Reading two · ten minutes
Psalm 78:1–8
From this week’s reading. We will not hide these things from our children, but tell the next generation. Read it as the job Psalm 71 asks for. Then ask what your family will tell its children.
Reading three · ten minutes
Mosiah 2:17–24
King Benjamin, an old man near the end of his reign, speaks to his whole people. He reminds them that God created them, preserves them day to day, and lends them breath. Read it as Psalm 71 preached to a nation.
✦ Study Tactics — Going to Another Level
Four Latter-day Saint study methods, sized for tonight
1 · Scripture journaling — twenty minutes, one page. Draw a line across the page for your life, from birth to today. Mark five moments when Heavenly Father helped you. At the end of the line, write Psalm 71:5.
2 · Footnote and Topical Guide chains — fifteen minutes. Follow the footnotes on Psalm 71:5 and 71:17. Then look up Hope and Premortal Life in the Topical Guide. Read Jeremiah 1:5, Abraham 3:22–23 and Doctrine and Covenants 138:56.
3 · Liken the scriptures — ten minutes, out loud. This week’s Come, Follow Me reading is Psalms 49–51; 61–66; 69–72; 77–78; 85–86. Read Psalm 71:17–18 with your child, then let them ask a grandparent what Heavenly Father has taught them since their youth.
4 · Pondering and recording impressions — five minutes, with an old photo. Hold a photo of yourself as a child. Ask: what did Heavenly Father see in that child that I have forgotten? Write what comes.
✦ One Invitation This Week
Tell one story of Heavenly Father’s care each day this week. One from your childhood, one from your child’s baby days, one from a grandparent. Short and true. Let the stories add up to a family Psalm 71.
On the seventh day, ask the FIND question from tonight’s Feeling Moment: who has taken care of you and loved you since you were a tiny baby? When they name the people, add one more name. Heavenly Father was there too.
