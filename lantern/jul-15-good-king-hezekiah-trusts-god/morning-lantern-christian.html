The Gospel: Hezekiah broke a bronze snake that Moses had made. Long before, in the wilderness, God told Moses to lift that snake on a pole. Anyone bitten by the venomous snakes could look at it and live. Jesus Himself said the snake pointed to Him. He would be lifted up on the cross, and everyone who believes in Him would have eternal life. By Hezekiah’s day, the people had forgotten what it pointed to. They burned incense to the snake itself. Trust Him.
📖 Tonight's Scripture Story
Good King Hezekiah Trusts God
Hezekiah became the king of Judah. He loved Heavenly Father with all his heart. The people had been worshiping pretend gods and idols. Hezekiah said, “We will follow the true God!”
He took away all the idols and false things. He helped the people remember Heavenly Father. Hezekiah trusted God more than any king before him. He held tight to the Lord and obeyed Him.
Because Hezekiah trusted God, Heavenly Father was with him. The Lord helped him and blessed the people. When troubles came, Hezekiah knew where to turn. He always looked to God first.
Trusting Heavenly Father means believing He will help us. It means choosing Him over everything else. When we trust God, He stays close to us. Trusting God is the best choice a heart can make.
★ Scripture Traveler of the Night
Beau the Brave
"Big heart, brave start!"
Beau fits tonight because trusting the Lord with your whole heart takes a big, brave heart. In Beau’s world today: Beau stands at the edge of the pool, toes curled over the side. Dad is in the water with his arms held out. Beau’s knees shake, but he remembers that Dad has never once let him sink. He takes a big breath and jumps, and Dad catches him. Just like King Hezekiah held tight to the Lord, Beau learned that trusting means jumping toward the One who loves you.
★ The Heart of Tonight's Story
Biblical Principle: Trusting the Lord means choosing Him first, and clearing away what pulls us from Him. Hezekiah did both. He took down the idols his father had set up. He broke the old bronze snake the people had started to worship. Then, when trouble came, he did not lean on Egypt or on money. He held fast to the Lord. The Bible says there was no king like him, before or after. His trust was not a feeling. It was a lifetime of choices.
Virtue: Trust & Faithfulness, because a child learns to trust God the same way they learn to trust a parent. They test it. They hold on, and the hand holds back. Tonight’s feeling is the steady feeling of a small hand in a big one. Every child knows it from crossing a busy street. Hezekiah held on to the Lord like that, and he did not let go.
For young children: King Hezekiah loved God. He took away the pretend gods and helped everyone remember the true God. When trouble came, he held tight to the Lord, like holding a grown-up’s hand. You can hold tight to God too. He will not let go.
✦ Verse of the Day

2 Kings 18:5 (New International Version)
Simple truth: Many kings in the Bible get a short line about what kind of king they were. Most of the lines are sad. This one is not. It names the one thing that made Hezekiah different: Hezekiah trusted in the Lord. Not his army. Not his walls. Not his gold. The next verse says how. He held fast to the Lord and did not stop following him. To hold fast means to hold on tight and not let go. That is what trust looks like in a life. It is a hand that keeps holding.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Tomorrow, when you hold your child’s hand to cross a street, say it out loud. This is what trusting God feels like. Let them squeeze. Squeeze back. Then pick one small thing in your own day that pulls you away from the Lord. Maybe it is the phone in the first ten minutes of the morning. Put it in another room tomorrow and say a prayer first instead. Hezekiah cleared away the things that pulled his people from God. A child learns that best by watching a parent do it.

Why This Story Matters
Every child will one day be told that trusting God is silly. A friend will say it, or a teacher, or a voice online. Tonight’s story gets them ready for that day. Hezekiah heard it too. An enemy commander stood outside the walls and shouted it up to the people. Do not let Hezekiah persuade you to trust in the Lord. The people did not answer. Hezekiah kept holding on.
Beau’s story is the same lesson at swimming-pool size. Beau stands on the edge with his knees shaking. Dad is in the water with his arms held out. Beau remembers that Dad has never once let him sink, so he jumps. Trust is not the absence of shaking knees. It is remembering who has always caught you, and jumping anyway.
The story also teaches a quieter lesson about clearing things away. Hezekiah took down idols, and even broke a bronze snake that had once been holy. Children have their own idols, though we rarely call them that. A screen, a toy, a need to win. The story gives you gentle words for this. Anything that pulls us away from God can be put away, so we can hold on to Him.
Historical Background
Hezekiah was twenty-five when he became king of Judah, the southern kingdom. He reigned twenty-nine years in Jerusalem. His father, Ahaz, had been one of the worst kings in the record. Ahaz copied a foreign altar for the temple and even sacrificed his own son in the fire. So Hezekiah grew up in a palace full of the very things he would later tear down.
In his first year as king, Hezekiah opened the doors of the temple and repaired them. 2 Chronicles 29 says the priests carried out everything unclean that had piled up inside. Then he sent letters all over the land, inviting everyone to come to Jerusalem for the Passover. Many laughed at the messengers. Some came, and the city had a joy it had not known since Solomon.
Meanwhile the north fell apart. In Hezekiah’s sixth year, Assyria captured Samaria and carried the people of Israel away. 2 Kings 18 says it happened because they had not obeyed the Lord. Eight years later, the same Assyrian army came south. King Sennacherib took the fortified towns of Judah one by one. Then he sent his commanders with a large army to the walls of Jerusalem itself.

Word Study — 2 Kings 18:4–6
Batach (בָּטַח) Dabaq (דָּבַק) Nechushtan (נְחֻשְׁתָּן)
Batach — “trusted” (v. 5). It means to lean your whole weight on something and feel safe. It is the word for resting on a sure thing. In chapter 18 this word comes back again and again, mostly from the enemy. The Assyrian commander keeps asking, in mockery, what Hezekiah is depending on. Tonight it matters because the whole chapter is one question. Who are you leaning on? Hezekiah’s answer was the Lord.
Dabaq — “held fast” (v. 6). It means to cling, to stick fast, to hold on like glue. It is the same word for a husband who is united to his wife, and for Ruth clinging to Naomi on the road. Hezekiah held on to the Lord like that. This is the word tonight’s Feeling Moment acts out. Grab a grown-up’s hand and do not let go.
Nechushtan — the name the bronze snake was called (v. 4). It sounds like the Hebrew words for bronze and for snake. In plain words, it was just a bronze snake thing. The people had made it holy. Hezekiah broke it. Tonight it matters because even good things can pull us from God if we start to trust them instead of Him.
★ The Snake Hezekiah Broke
A holy thing that had stopped pointing to the Lord
Numbers 21 · 2 Kings 18 · John 3
The bronze snake has a long story. In Numbers 21, venomous snakes bit the people of Israel in the wilderness. The Lord told Moses to make a snake and put it up on a pole. Anyone who was bitten could look at it and live. It was a real miracle and a holy object. Jesus Himself said He would be lifted up the same way, so that everyone who believes in Him may have eternal life. It is the verse just before John 3:16.
Hundreds of years later, the snake was still around, and people were burning incense to it. They had forgotten what it pointed to. They were trusting the bronze instead of the Lord. So Hezekiah broke it into pieces. That took courage. It was old and famous and had been made by Moses. But a thing that had once pointed to the Lord now stood in His way. For a family, the lesson is gentle and real. Good things are good only while they point us to Jesus.
The Line to Carry Out of Tonight
"Trusting Heavenly Father means believing He loves you and will help you."
— Remember This, from tonight's story in Little Scripture Travelers
Notice the order of the line. First, He loves you. Then, He will help you. A child cannot trust someone they are not sure loves them. So trust starts with love. When your child is afraid, start there. Remind them first that God loves them. Then remind them that He helps. The Remember page ends with a promise a child can hold on to: He is always there for you.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Start with what Hezekiah did first. Before any trouble came, he cleared away the idols. He did not wait for an army at the gate to decide whom he trusted. The choosing happened on ordinary days. So pick one ordinary thing this week to clear away. It might be a show, an app, or a habit that crowds out prayer. Put it away for seven days and see what fills the space.
Then answer the Parent’s Heart question about trust with a real story. When did you lean on the Lord recently? Tell your child in two or three sentences. Children think trust is something grown-ups already have. When they hear that you had to choose it this month, they learn that trust is a choice they can make too.
And tell the ancestor story if you have one. The Parent’s Heart asks about someone in your line who put God first. Maybe they came to faith against their family’s wishes. Maybe they crossed an ocean to worship freely. Tell it simply, and end with Hezekiah’s words: they held fast to the Lord. Your child will hear that faith runs in families, and that they can hold on too.

The Full Passage in Context
What comes before. Chapter 16 tells of King Ahaz, Hezekiah’s father, who brought foreign worship into Jerusalem. Chapter 17 tells how the northern kingdom fell to Assyria and was carried away. The writer stops to explain why. The people had turned from the Lord, again and again, though prophets warned them. That is the dark background for chapter 18. Hezekiah comes to the throne as the north disappears.
How the chapter builds. Verses 1 to 8 are Hezekiah’s good beginning. He does what is right, removes the high places, breaks the bronze snake, and trusts the Lord. Verses 9 to 12 retell the fall of Samaria. Verses 13 to 16 are hard. Sennacherib takes Judah’s towns, and Hezekiah pays him with silver and the gold from the temple doors. Verses 17 to 37 bring the Assyrian commanders to the walls. The field commander shouts in Hebrew so the people can hear. Who are you depending on? The people stay silent, as the king commanded.
What comes after. In chapter 19 Hezekiah tears his clothes and goes into the temple of the Lord. He sends for the prophet Isaiah. When a threatening letter arrives, he spreads it out before the Lord and prays. That is the next story in this book. The Lord answers through Isaiah, and Jerusalem is saved in one night. The whole story is told again in Isaiah 36 and 37.
Second Word Study — 2 Kings 18:3, 4, 19
Yashar (יָשָׁר) Bamoth (בָּמוֹת) Bittachon (בִּטָּחוֹן)
Yashar — “right” (v. 3). It means straight, level, not bent. A straight path, an upright person. The Bible says Hezekiah did what was right in the eyes of the Lord, like David. Notice whose eyes. Not the people’s. Not the neighbors’. The Lord’s. Tonight it matters because a child can learn early that the question is not what friends think. It is what God sees.
Bamoth — “high places” (v. 4). These were hilltop shrines all over the land. Some were for idols. Some were for the Lord, but in ways He had not asked for. Kings before Hezekiah left them alone because they were popular. Hezekiah took them down. It cost him, and the enemy even mocked him for it in verse 22. Clearing away what is popular takes a brave heart.
Bittachon — “confidence” (v. 19). It comes from the same root as batach, to trust. The commander asks, On what are you basing this confidence of yours? He meant it as a sneer. But it is a fair question for every family. What is our confidence? Hezekiah’s answer came in the next chapter. He took the threat to the temple and laid it before the Lord.

Commentary — What Has Been Taught
Readers have long noticed how high the praise is in verse 5. No king before or after was like Hezekiah in trust. A few chapters later, Josiah gets similar praise for turning to the Lord with all his heart. Many readers see no conflict. Hezekiah stood out for his trust, and Josiah stood out for turning back to the law. Each one was the best at something.
The Bible is also honest that Hezekiah was not perfect. In verses 14 to 16 he panicked and paid Assyria with gold stripped from the temple doors. Later, in chapter 20, he showed his treasures to visitors from Babylon and was warned for it. Scripture does not hide this. Trust is not the same as never failing. It is turning back to the Lord when the next trouble comes, as Hezekiah did in chapter 19.
Spurgeon, on tonight’s sources card, gives a picture that fits tonight’s Feeling Moment. A blind man leans on his friend’s arm and gets home safely. That is trust. Where faithful readers differ. Some see the payment to Assyria as a lack of faith. Others see a careful king buying time. Either way, the chapter ends with Hezekiah’s people silent and holding on.
A Biblical Thread — Holding Fast to the Lord
Deuteronomy 10:20. Moses tells Israel to fear the Lord, serve Him, and hold fast to Him. The word Hezekiah will live out begins as a command.
Joshua 23:8. Joshua, old and near the end, begs the people to keep holding fast to the Lord as they have done.
2 Kings 18:5–6. Tonight. Hezekiah trusted the Lord and held fast to Him. No king of Judah was like him.
Psalm 56:3–4. David writes the child-sized version. When I am afraid, I put my trust in you.
Proverbs 3:5–6. Trust in the Lord with all your heart. The verse many families teach first.
Jeremiah 17:7–8. The one who trusts the Lord is like a tree by a stream. Its roots reach the water, and it keeps bearing fruit in a dry year.
John 15:4–5. Jesus says to remain in Him, like a branch joined to a vine. Holding fast to the Lord becomes staying close to Jesus.
Hebrews 12:1–2. Throw off what hinders and fix your eyes on Jesus. The thread ends with Hezekiah’s two moves: clear away, and hold on.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Start with what Hezekiah did first. Before any trouble came, he cleared away the idols. He did not wait for an army at the gate to decide whom he trusted. The choosing happened on ordinary days. So pick one ordinary thing this week to clear away. It might be a show, an app, or a habit that crowds out prayer. Put it away for seven days and see what fills the space.
Then answer the Parent’s Heart question about trust with a real story. When did you lean on the Lord recently? Tell your child in two or three sentences. Children think trust is something grown-ups already have. When they hear that you had to choose it this month, they learn that trust is a choice they can make too.
And tell the ancestor story if you have one. The Parent’s Heart asks about someone in your line who put God first. Maybe they came to faith against their family’s wishes. Maybe they crossed an ocean to worship freely. Tell it simply, and end with Hezekiah’s words: they held fast to the Lord. Your child will hear that faith runs in families, and that they can hold on too.

✦ Connecting the Dots
Where this truth begins, how it grows, and how it reaches your child’s bed
The idea in tonight’s story begins in the wilderness with Moses. He tells Israel to hold fast to the Lord, to hold on and not let go. He also lifts up a bronze snake so bitten people can look and live. Both pictures say the same thing. Life comes from looking to the Lord and holding on to Him.
Over the years the people let go. They built high places and set up idols. They even began to worship the bronze snake. Then a young king named Hezekiah, raised by a father who worshiped false gods, chose differently. He cleared the idols away and broke the snake. He trusted the Lord, and when an enemy army came, he held on.
Jesus gathered both pictures into Himself. He said that as Moses lifted up the snake, so would He be lifted up. The cross is the place to look and live. He also invited us to remain in Him, joined to Him like a branch to a vine. Holding on to the Lord became holding on to Jesus.
The letters of the New Testament carry the call forward. Hebrews tells us to throw off what hinders and fix our eyes on Jesus. John ends his letter with a word to his dear children: keep yourselves from idols. Peter tells us to cast every worry on Him because He cares. The hand is still held out.
So it arrives at your child’s bed in a form small enough to use tonight. Trusting God means believing He loves you and will help you. When you are scared, hold on tight. Put away anything that pulls you from Him. He is always there for you.

✦ Three Passages to Sit With
Longer readings matched to tonight's text
Reading one · fifteen minutes
2 Kings 18 and 19, read straight through
Read both chapters at one sitting. Chapter 18 is the threat. Chapter 19 is the answer. You will hear the enemy shout again and again, Who are you depending on? Then you will watch Hezekiah walk into the temple with the threatening letter and spread it out before the Lord. Reading them together shows what trust looks like on the worst day of a king’s life.
Reading two · ten minutes
2 Chronicles 29 to 30
The other side of Hezekiah’s story. In his first month as king he opens the temple and has it cleaned out. Then he invites the whole land to Passover, even the scattered north. Many laugh at the messengers, but some come. Read it to see that Hezekiah’s trust was built on ordinary days, long before the army came.
Reading three · five minutes
Psalm 46
God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in trouble. The psalm pictures a city with enemies all around and God in the middle of it. Some readers hear Jerusalem in Hezekiah’s day in it. Whether or not it was written then, it is the song of a trusting heart. Read it slowly and stop at the line, Be still, and know that I am God.
✦ Study Tactics — Going to Another Level
Four Bible study methods, sized for tonight
1 · SOAP journaling — twenty minutes, one page. Scripture, Observation, Application, Prayer. Copy 2 Kings 18:5–6 by hand. Circle the three things Hezekiah did: trusted, held fast, kept. Beside each one, write one way you did that this month, or one way you want to. End with a short prayer asking God to help you hold on.
2 · Two translations — fifteen minutes. Read verses 5 to 7 in the NIV and then in an older translation like the King James. One says held fast, the other clave. In verse 19, one asks on what are you basing this confidence, the other what confidence is this. Write down what each wording helps you see.
3 · Praying the passage — ten minutes, out loud. Read verses 19 to 22 aloud, where the commander mocks Hezekiah’s trust. Then name the voices in your own life that ask the same question. Turn each one into a prayer. Lord, when I hear this, help me hold fast to You.
4 · Memorize and record — five minutes, tonight, before you sleep. Say Proverbs 3:5–6 three times until it stays. Then ask one question: what is my bronze snake? Is there a good thing I have started to trust more than the Lord? Write down the first thing that comes, and one small step to put it back in its place.
✦ One Invitation This Week
Hold on, all week. Every time you hold your child’s hand this week, give it one extra squeeze. Tell them it means God is holding on to you. Let them squeeze back to say I am holding on to Him. By the end of the week it will be a family signal. It will work in a crowded store, at the doctor, and on the first day of school.
