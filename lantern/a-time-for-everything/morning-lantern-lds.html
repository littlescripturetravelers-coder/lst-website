Week 2 · Night 5 · A Time for Everything
★ A Parent's Heart for Tonight
Before the lights go low
1. When did trusting Heavenly Father’s timing bring you peace? Share a season you’re grateful you waited through.
2. How do you help your child accept that feelings and seasons pass? Tell what steadies you.
3. Did a grandparent or ancestor live with patient trust in Heavenly Father’s timing? Describe their steadiness.
The Atonement: Read the poem in Ecclesiastes 3 slowly and notice what it refuses to do: it will not pretend. A time to be born, and a time to die. A time to weep. A time to lose. Most comfort offered to children edits that list down to the cheerful half, and children can tell. This chapter does not, and that is precisely why it can be trusted at bedtime — it tells the truth about the hard times and then says Heavenly Father is holding those too. And the reason a Latter-day Saint family can say that without flinching is that the Savior did not stay outside the list. He came in the meridian of time and took a body that could be tired and a heart that could grieve; at Lazarus’s grave, knowing exactly what He was about to do, He wept first. Jesus Christ has been in every season on that page, including the one your child is most afraid of. Say it plainly: “There is a time for everything — and Jesus has been in every one of them, so He knows how to stay with you.”
📖 Tonight's Scripture Story
A Time for Everything
A wise teacher once wrote a thoughtful book called Ecclesiastes. In it, he shared a gentle truth: there is a right time for everything.
“There’s a time to plant seeds and a time to pick the fruit,” he said. “A time to laugh and a time to cry. A time to hug and a time to rest.”
Heavenly Father made the world with seasons and times. Winter comes, then spring. Morning comes, then night. Everything has its own special time.
Some days are for playing and giggling. Some days are for resting and being quiet. Some feelings come and go, and that’s okay — each one has its time.
Heavenly Father is with us in every season and every time. We can trust Him whether we’re laughing or crying, busy or still. He holds all our times in His loving hands.
★ Scripture Traveler of the Night
Wren the Wonderer
"Pause and ponder — what a wonder!"
Wren fits tonight because she thinks about the right time for things. In Wren’s world today: Wren wanted to play outside, but it was pouring rain. Instead of fussing, she wondered, “Maybe this is a time to stay cozy inside.” So she read books and built a fort, and when the sun came out later, it was the perfect time to splash in puddles. Just like the wise teacher said there’s a time for everything, Wren learned to trust each season’s time.
★ The Heart of Tonight's Story
Gospel Principle: Time is not a mess a family is left to survive; it is something Heavenly Father made, on purpose, with seasons in it. The Preacher does not tell his reader to make life hold still. He tells him there is a season for every thing, and a time to every purpose under heaven — and then lists the hard half of it out loud. Peace here does not come from getting only the good times. It comes from believing that the One who set the times is present in all of them.
Virtue: Peace & Trust — because a child who believes that a sad afternoon has a time and an end will not need the afternoon to be over in order to be all right. Tonight’s feeling word from the Five Stones is peaceful, and it is the settled, it-is-all-okay feeling of someone who is not fighting the clock.
For young children: There is a right time for everything. A time to play and a time to rest. A time to laugh and a time to cry. Heavenly Father made all the times, and He is with you in every single one.
✦ Verse of the Day
"To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven."
Ecclesiastes 3:1 (King James Version)
Simple truth: Two words carry the verse. Season — a set time, the kind a farmer waits for and cannot argue with. And purpose, which is the surprising one: the Hebrew behind it is closer to delight or matter at hand than to destiny. Put them together and the verse is not saying that everything happens for a reason. It is saying that everything has its own hour, and that the hours were appointed by Someone. ✦ Apply It — For You, Today
The next time your child is crying about something small, try not ending it. Do not fix it, distract from it, or explain why it is not worth crying about — just sit there and say, this is a sad minute; sad minutes have an end. Then stay until it does. Ecclesiastes 3 is a whole chapter arguing that the hard seasons are real and temporary, and the only way a small child learns that is by being held through one instead of being talked out of it.

Why This Story Matters
Small children do not yet know that feelings end. That is not a figure of speech — it is a developmental fact, and it is the reason a three-year-old cries as though the disappointment is permanent. It is permanent, as far as they can tell. Everything you do tonight is teaching the one thing that makes childhood survivable: this has a time, and the time will pass, and you will still be here afterwards, and so will I.
Ecclesiastes 3 teaches it in the least sentimental way in all of scripture. The poem does not say the sad things are secretly good. It sets them in pairs — born and die, weep and laugh, embrace and refrain — and refuses to rank them. A child hearing tonight’s story learns that crying is not a failure of a good day; it is one of the times, it has its hour, and it is not a sign that something has gone wrong with them.
Then the story does what the chapter does next, which is the part that turns information into comfort. Heavenly Father is with us in every season and every time. Not above the seasons, watching them go by — in them. That is the sentence to say twice tonight, because a child who believes it has somewhere to put every feeling they have not learned the name of yet.
Historical Background
The book calls its author Qoheleth, usually translated the Preacher — a word built from the Hebrew for assembly, so something nearer to the one who gathers or the speaker to the congregation. The opening verse names him the son of David, king in Jerusalem, and the tradition that stands behind tonight’s story gives the book to Solomon in his later years. Whoever held the pen, the voice is unmistakably that of a man who has had everything and is telling you, calmly, what it was worth.
Ecclesiastes belongs to what scholars call the wisdom books — Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes — and it is the one that asks the awkward questions. Proverbs tells you how life generally works. Job asks why it did not work for one man. Ecclesiastes stands back and looks at the whole thing under the sun, notices how little of it a person controls, and says so, over and over, without ever letting go of God.
The poem in chapter 3 is built on a device Hebrew poetry loves: paired opposites that mean and everything in between. Fourteen pairs, twenty-eight times, from birth to death and war to peace. To an ancient ear that construction says all of it — the way we say high and low and mean everybody. The chapter is not a checklist of situations. It is the whole of a human life, laid out in fourteen lines, and put under the heading of appointed time.

Word Study — Ecclesiastes 3:1
Zeman (זְמָן) Eth (עֵת) Chephets (חֵפֶץ)
Zeman — the word behind season. It means an appointed time, a time that has been set, and it is a late word in Hebrew: the same root turns up in Aramaic and in the parts of scripture written after the exile. The point is not that the time feels right. It is that the time was fixed, by somebody, before anyone arrived to have an opinion about it.
Eth — the word repeated twenty-eight times through the poem, translated time. This is the ordinary word for an occasion, the right moment for a thing: the time of harvest, the time of rain, the time when kings go out to battle. It is the word tonight’s story hands a child in its simplest form — everything has its own special time.
Chephets — translated purpose in the King James text, and this is where the famous verse is most often misread. Elsewhere in scripture the word means delight, pleasure, the matter one has in hand. The verse is not promising that every event has a hidden reason. It is saying that every undertaking, every piece of business a person has, comes with an hour that fits it. That is a gentler claim, and a truer one to say over a child who has just been told no.
★ Where the Preacher Says It Again
The same idea, three more times, across the book
Ecclesiastes 3:11 · 7:14 · 8:6 · 9:11
Chapter 3 does not stop at the poem, and the line that follows it is the one most worth carrying into a bedroom: “He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end.” Beautiful in his time — not in ours, and not yet. Then the Preacher adds the ache that makes the verse honest: something eternal has been set in us, and it is exactly what makes the waiting hard.
He returns to it. In chapter 7 he tells a reader to be joyful in the day of prosperity and to consider in the day of adversity, and says plainly that God has set the one over against the other. In chapter 8 he observes that every purpose has its time and judgement, and that a man’s trouble is heavy upon him because he does not know what is coming. In chapter 9 he watches the race go to someone other than the swift and calls it time and chance. Read the four together and you get the shape of the whole book: the times are real, they are not ours to schedule, and the man who knows this is the one who can finally rest.
The Line to Carry Out of Tonight
"There's a right time for everything — a time to play and a time to rest — and Heavenly Father is with you in every one!"
— Remember This, from tonight's story in Little Scripture Travelers
Notice the grammar of the promise. It does not say Heavenly Father will bring the good time back, or that the right time is coming soon. It says He is with you in every one — present tense, all of them, including this one. That is the only version of this comfort a child can actually use at half past seven when the day has gone wrong. Then ask the Remember This question as they settle, and let the answer be small: what is something this is the right time for tonight? A hug. A story. Sleeping. Naming the right thing for this hour is the entire practice of Ecclesiastes 3, done at child scale.

"What are some of the times the teacher talked about?"

💬 Go-Deeper Conversation
"The poem names a time to weep and a time to lose without apologising for either. Which season am I currently treating as a mistake to be fixed rather than a time to be lived?"
"Ecclesiastes 3:11 says He has made every thing beautiful in his time. Where am I asking Heavenly Father to move up a date rather than asking Him to stay with me until it comes?"
"Wren wondered whether the rain might be a time to stay cozy inside. What in this week is actually an invitation to a different season rather than an obstacle to the one I had planned?"
✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Read the poem out loud once, the whole of verses 1 to 8, at adult speed and without flinching at the line about dying. It takes ninety seconds. Most of us have only ever met this passage in fragments — on a plaque, at a funeral, in a song — and the fragments are always the pleasant ones. Hearing all fourteen pairs in one breath does something a summary cannot: it stops the chapter from sounding like an excuse and starts it sounding like an honest description of the life you are actually living.
Then name the season you are in, out loud, to one other adult, in one sentence. Not things are a bit much. This is a season of caring for my mother, and it will not be forever. The Preacher’s comfort works by putting a boundary around a stretch of time. A season that has been named has edges, and a thing with edges can be carried; an unnamed hard stretch quietly becomes your whole life.
And keep the child-sized version in reach for tomorrow, because that is where this chapter earns its keep. When a small disaster arrives — the wrong cup, the rained-off park, the friend who went home — say the sentence from tonight’s story before you say anything clever: some feelings come and go, and that’s okay; each one has its time. Repeat it enough weeks and a child learns to say it to themselves, which is the whole inheritance.
🌙 Tonight's Story Connection
In tonight’s Little Scripture Travelers story, Wren the Wonderer gets Ecclesiastes 3 in one afternoon. She wanted to play outside and it was pouring. The hinge of the story is not that she cheered up — it is one wondering sentence: maybe this is a time to stay cozy inside. Books and a fort, and then the sun came out and it was the perfect time to splash in puddles. Both halves of the day were a time. Neither was the day going wrong.
Tonight’s Five Stones do the naming for you. SPOT: the wise teacher felt calm, knowing everything has its right time. NAME: that settled, it’s-all-okay feeling is called peaceful. And the FORK is the whole lesson in one choice — we can fuss when it’s not the time we want, or trust that Heavenly Father knows the right time. Ask the FIND question exactly as it is written and then wait for the answer: when did waiting for the right time make something even better?
Do the Feeling Moment with the whole body, because this one moves through the times rather than talking about them: plant a tiny seed, reach up tall as it grows, a big ha ha! of a laugh, then one slow calm breath. Show me a time to jump, and a time to be still. Finish on the CALM stone — three slow breaths, and a whisper: Heavenly Father holds all my times. Let that be the last thing said before the light goes off.

The Full Passage in Context
What comes before. Chapters 1 and 2 are the Preacher’s experiment, and it fails on purpose. He tries wisdom, pleasure, building projects, gardens, music, silver and gold, and at the end of each he writes the same verdict: vanity, and vexation of spirit. Chapter 3 opens straight out of that exhaustion. It matters enormously that the famous poem about time arrives after a man has proved he cannot make life add up on his own. It is not the opening optimism of the book. It is what he had left when the optimism ran out.
How the chapter builds. Verses 1 to 8 are the poem, fourteen pairs, no commentary. Verse 9 asks the question the poem provokes — what profit has the worker in his labour? Verses 10 and 11 answer it: God has given people this business to be exercised in, and He has made every thing beautiful in his time, and set the world in their heart so that no one can find out His work from the beginning to the end. Verses 12 and 13 land somewhere surprisingly domestic: rejoice, do good, eat and drink and enjoy the good of your labour, for it is the gift of God. Verse 14 closes the argument — whatsoever God does shall be for ever, and nothing can be added to it or taken from it.
What comes after. The rest of the chapter turns to what the Preacher sees under the sun: judgement where justice should have been, and the plain fact that people and animals alike return to the dust. He does not soften it. The book will go on asking these questions for nine more chapters before its last two verses gather everything into one instruction — fear God and keep His commandments. Chapter 3 is where the book first finds its footing, and the footing is this: I cannot see the whole work, and the One who can has set the times.
Second Word Study — Ecclesiastes 3:11
Olam (עוֹלָם) Yapheh (יָפֶה) Hevel (הֶבֶל)
Olam — the word the King James translators rendered the world in verse 11, and one of the most argued-over words in the book. Its usual sense across the Old Testament is not world but everlasting, age, time out of mind. He has set olam in their heart. Something long, something without a visible edge, planted inside people who live about seventy years — which is exactly why a child can grieve the end of a day as though it were the end of everything, and why an adult still aches at the passing of good things.
Yapheh — beautiful, in the phrase beautiful in his time. It is a plain, physical word: a well-made thing, a lovely face, a fitting shape. Not meaningful, not worth it in the end. The claim of the verse is aesthetic and it is patient. Each thing is fitting when its hour comes, and the fitting-ness may only be visible from a distance nobody standing in the season can reach.
Hevel — the drumbeat of the whole book, translated vanity. It means breath, vapour, the mist you can see in the cold and cannot hold. Read vanity of vanities as breath of breaths and the Preacher stops sounding like a cynic: he is not saying life is worthless, he is saying it will not be grasped. That is the problem chapter 3 answers. If your days are vapour in your own hands, the only rest available is that they are not only in your hands.

Commentary — What Has Been Taught
Ecclesiastes has always made readers slightly nervous, and the record of that nervousness is part of its value. Ancient rabbinic discussion records a real debate about whether the book belonged among the sacred writings at all; the argument was settled in its favour, and the traditional reading treats its bluntness as a mercy rather than a threat. Christian readers through the centuries have generally taken the same route as the book’s own last two verses: read the whole of it, and let the conclusion — fear God, keep His commandments — hold the rest in place.
Latter-day Saint teaching has tended to read chapter 3 alongside the revelations on time and seasons rather than on its own. The doctrine that all things move in their times and seasons by law, and that time is measured unto men rather than unto God, puts the Preacher’s frustration in a frame he could not have had: he is not watching a world that runs on nothing. He is watching one whose schedule belongs to Somebody, from the inside, which is exactly where every parent reading this is standing tonight.
Where faithful readers differ. Authorship is genuinely disputed — the Hebrew of the book carries late features, and many careful readers take son of David as a literary voice rather than a signature. Readers also differ over verse 11’s olam: eternity set in the heart, or a sense of the whole span of time, or something closer to the puzzle of the ages. None of it changes what the chapter asks of a family. Every reading agrees that the times are appointed, that we cannot see the whole, and that the right response is to receive today as a gift and not to demand tomorrow’s timetable.
A Doctrinal Thread — The Times Are Appointed
Genesis 1:14. Lights in the firmament, and the first job given them is to divide the day from the night and to be for signs and for seasons, for days and years. Time is not the backdrop of the creation; it is one of the things made, and it was called good before any person had to live inside it.
Genesis 8:22. After the flood, the promise that seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night shall not cease. The first thing Heavenly Father guarantees a frightened family is that the seasons will keep turning.
Ecclesiastes 3. Tonight. The fourteen pairs, the question about profit, and the answer in verse 11 — beautiful in his time, with eternity set in the heart of the one who has to wait.
Psalm 31:15. The poem turned into a prayer, by a man in danger. My times are in thy hand. Not my life in general: my times, the specific hours I cannot control.
Galatians 4:4. The fulness of the time — the appointed hour of the whole plan, kept by Heavenly Father across centuries, and arriving as a baby. Every family that has ever waited too long for something has this to hold.
Alma 40:8–10. Time is measured only unto men, and God knows all the times appointed unto man. The Book of Mormon addressing the exact anxiety Ecclesiastes raises and naming the One who holds the calendar.
Doctrine and Covenants 88:42–45. A law given to all things, and every body moving in its times and its seasons. The thread closes where it began, in the created order — and a child who learns tonight that bedtime has its hour is learning the smallest true version of it.
The Line to Carry Out of Tonight
"There's a right time for everything — a time to play and a time to rest — and Heavenly Father is with you in every one!"
— Remember This, from tonight's story in Little Scripture Travelers
Notice the grammar of the promise. It does not say Heavenly Father will bring the good time back, or that the right time is coming soon. It says He is with you in every one — present tense, all of them, including this one. That is the only version of this comfort a child can actually use at half past seven when the day has gone wrong. Then ask the Remember This question as they settle, and let the answer be small: what is something this is the right time for tonight? A hug. A story. Sleeping. Naming the right thing for this hour is the entire practice of Ecclesiastes 3, done at child scale.

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Read the poem out loud once, the whole of verses 1 to 8, at adult speed and without flinching at the line about dying. It takes ninety seconds. Most of us have only ever met this passage in fragments — on a plaque, at a funeral, in a song — and the fragments are always the pleasant ones. Hearing all fourteen pairs in one breath does something a summary cannot: it stops the chapter from sounding like an excuse and starts it sounding like an honest description of the life you are actually living.
Then name the season you are in, out loud, to one other adult, in one sentence. Not things are a bit much. This is a season of caring for my mother, and it will not be forever. The Preacher’s comfort works by putting a boundary around a stretch of time. A season that has been named has edges, and a thing with edges can be carried; an unnamed hard stretch quietly becomes your whole life.
And keep the child-sized version in reach for tomorrow, because that is where this chapter earns its keep. When a small disaster arrives — the wrong cup, the rained-off park, the friend who went home — say the sentence from tonight’s story before you say anything clever: some feelings come and go, and that’s okay; each one has its time. Repeat it enough weeks and a child learns to say it to themselves, which is the whole inheritance.

Three Questions to Sit With This Week
1. The poem gives equal weight to the weeping and the laughing. Which of the two do I treat, in practice, as the real life and which as the interruption — and what would change in my house if I stopped?

✦ Connecting the Dots
Where this doctrine begins, how it grows, and how it reaches your kitchen table
It begins on the fourth day of the creation. Lights are set in the heavens and given a job: divide the day from the night, and be for signs, for seasons, for days and years. Before there is a person to be impatient, there is time, and it is made, and it is called good. Everything Ecclesiastes says later stands on that. The turning of things is not the world wearing out. It is the world working.
It is guaranteed after the flood. Seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter shall not cease — the first covenant promise made to a family who had just watched everything they knew go under water. Notice what is promised. Not that nothing hard will happen again; that the seasons will keep coming round. A great deal of biblical comfort turns out to be of that shape.
Then Ecclesiastes stress-tests it. A man who has tried everything writes down what it is like to live inside appointed times you did not appoint and cannot see the end of. His conclusion is not cheerful and it is not despairing: everything is beautiful in its time, eternity is set in our hearts, we cannot find out the whole work — so eat your bread, do good, and take the day as the gift it is. That is the most honest thing said about time anywhere in scripture.
The New Testament supplies the proof. When the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth His Son. The whole plan turned on an appointed hour, kept across centuries, missed by almost everybody who was waiting for it. And the revelations of the Restoration put the same idea back into the created order — a law given to all things, each moving in its times and its seasons, and time itself measured unto men rather than unto God.
So it arrives at your kitchen table tonight in a sentence a four-year-old can hold: everything has its own special time, and Heavenly Father is with you in all of them. A time to play. A time to rest. A time to cry, which will also end. You are not teaching your child to like the hard seasons. You are teaching them that the hard seasons have edges, and Somebody is holding both of them.

Appointed times, traced across the standard works
Ten references, described in our own words rather than quoted, except where the wording was read at its source tonight. The descriptions are ours; the words are not. Open them and see.

Psalm 31:15 — My times are in thy hand. The poem turned into a prayer by a man being hunted.

Doctrine and Covenants 88:42–45 — A law given to all things, and every body moving in its times and its seasons, giving light one to another.
Read them in order and watch the argument build. Genesis makes the seasons and promises them; the Psalms pray inside them; the prophets wait through them; Ecclesiastes admits how hard the waiting is; the letters announce that the great appointed hour arrived exactly on time. Nothing in that sequence tells a family that the hard season is short. All of it says the season is held.

✦ Study Tactics — Going to Another Level
Four Latter-day Saint study methods, sized for tonight
1 · Scripture journaling — twenty minutes, two columns. Copy the fourteen pairs of Ecclesiastes 3:2–8 down the page, one pair per line. In the second column, beside each, write the date of the last time you were in that season — a birth, a loss, a planting, a letting go. Most pages come out fuller than the writer expected. It is a quiet way of noticing that you have already lived through more appointed times than you remember.
2 · Footnote and Topical Guide chains — fifteen minutes. Open Ecclesiastes 3 in your own scriptures and follow the footnotes out of verses 1 and 11, then read the Topical Guide entries for Time and for Patience rather than skimming them. Chains built from the printed helps are steadier than a search, and tonight they will carry you into the Psalms and the prophets without being told to.
3 · Liken the scriptures — ten minutes, out loud. Read verses 1 to 8 aloud, and after each pair say the season of your own household it names. A time to plant: the thing we started this year. A time to keep silence: the conversation I am not having yet. Nephi’s instruction was to liken all scripture to ourselves, and this passage was practically built for it.
4 · Pondering and recording impressions — five minutes, tonight, before you sleep. Ask one question — what season am I actually in? — and write what comes without tidying it into something respectable. Then write the date. A family that keeps these finds out years later that the season they were sure would never end lasted eleven months.
✦ One Invitation This Week
Give every person in the house a season to name and a season to wait well in. At dinner one night, go round the table: each person names the season they are in, in one sentence, and nobody is allowed to fix anybody. Then each person names one thing that is the right time for this week — a letter to write, a friend to call, a thing to finally put down. Ecclesiastes is a book about knowing what hour it is; a household that says the hour out loud is a household practising it.
Then, on the seventh day, ask each person the FIND question from tonight’s Feeling Moment: when did waiting for the right time make something even better? Write the answers on one page, date it, and put it somewhere you will find it in a year. The Preacher’s complaint is that no one can see the work from the beginning to the end. A page of small waits that turned out well is the nearest thing a family gets to a view from above.
