★ A Parent's Heart for Tonight
Before the lights go low
1. When have you felt your family was a sacred gift from Heavenly Father? Share that tender awareness.
2. How do you invite Heavenly Father to help “build your house”? Tell about a way He’s strengthened your home.
3. Did a grandparent or ancestor build a home of faith and love? Describe the family blessing they began.
The Atonement: The psalm says the house is built by the Lord or it is not built at all, and then it says children are His heritage — not ours. Both halves are about ownership. The child asleep down the hall belongs to Heavenly Father first, and He has lent her to you. That is a relief and a weight at once, and the Atonement of Jesus Christ is what makes it bearable. The Savior is the one Builder who has already paid for every crack in the walls; the mistakes you made at dinner tonight are inside His reach, and so is the child who watched you make them. When the psalmist says he giveth his beloved sleep, he is describing a house where someone else is keeping watch. Say it plainly: “You are a gift from Heavenly Father, and Jesus loves you even more than we do.” Then go to sleep. The Builder is awake.

📖 Tonight's Scripture Story
Children Are a Gift
Heavenly Father loves families very much. One of the Psalms is a happy song all about home and children.
“Unless Heavenly Father helps build the house, the builders work for nothing,” the song says. That means Heavenly Father is the one who helps our family and home grow strong and happy.
Then the song says something sweet: “Children are a gift from Heavenly Father!” Every child — including YOU — is a special present He gives to a family.
The song paints a happy picture: a mom and dad and their little ones gathered close, like young plants growing round a table full of love.
Your family is a gift, and you are a gift too! Heavenly Father helps your home be a place of love, where you are treasured every single day.
★ Scripture Traveler of the Night
Willow the Warm
"Warm hugs, warm hearts!"
Willow fits tonight because she cherishes the people in her home. In Willow’s world today: at dinner, Willow looked around at everyone laughing together and felt her heart go warm. “I’m so glad you’re my family,” she said, and gave her little brother a hug — even though he’d been silly all day. Just like the song about children being a gift from Heavenly Father, Willow felt how precious her family is.
★ The Heart of Tonight's Story
Gospel Principle: A home is something Heavenly Father builds, and children are something He gives. Psalm 127 puts those two sentences side by side on purpose: the builders labour in vain unless the Lord builds, and children are an heritage of the Lord — an inheritance, family land, the thing you hold for the next generation. The family is not a project we finish. It is a gift we receive, and then a house we let Him build.
Virtue: Gratitude & Belonging — because a child who knows she is a gift, and not an accident or a chore, has been handed something no report card can take away. Tonight's feeling word is treasured, and it is a bigger word than liked. Liked means someone enjoys you today. Treasured means someone would keep you at any cost, and already has.
For young children: Heavenly Father helps build our home. He gave me to my family as a gift, and He gave my family to me. I am treasured. I can say thank You for the people in my house.
✦ Verse of the Day
"Lo, children are an heritage of the Lord: and the fruit of the womb is his reward."
Psalm 127:3 (KJV) ↗
Simple truth: Read the first word. Lo — look, behold, pay attention. The psalmist is pointing at something in the room that everyone has stopped noticing: the children. Then two nouns. Heritage is an inheritance, the land a family holds and hands down. Reward is wages, something paid out on purpose. A child, says the psalm, is not a cost or a chance. She is an inheritance and a payment, both from Heavenly Father, and both given — which means the right response is not pride, and not worry, but thanks. (Verse 1, in the same psalm: Except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it.)

✦ Apply It — For You, Today
Tomorrow, before anyone leaves the house, say one sentence to one child that begins with I am so glad you are — and finish it with something true and specific. I am so glad you are the one who notices when the dog is sad. I am so glad you are in this family. Not praise for a performance; thanks for a person. Children learn that they are a gift by hearing an adult receive them like one. It takes eight seconds, and it will outlast most of what you say this week.

Why This Story Matters
Every child eventually asks, in some wordless way, whether she is wanted. Not whether she is fed or driven to practice — whether she was a good idea. The whole question of belonging is what a child concludes about that, and she concludes it early, from a thousand small moments at a table. Tonight's psalm answers the question before it is asked, in a picture a two-year-old can hold: children are a gift, a present Heavenly Father gives to a family. Willow's story is that answer in miniature. She looked around the dinner table, felt her heart go warm, and said it out loud to her brother, silly day and all.
That is the quiet claim of Psalm 127, and it deserves to be said plainly to a family. A home is not chiefly a thing parents build by effort. Verse 1 rules that out — the builders labour in vain unless the Lord builds. A home is a thing Heavenly Father builds, and a child is a thing He gives, and the parents' work is to receive both with thanks and keep the doors open. That is not an excuse for laziness; verse 4 has arrows and a warrior in it. But it is a relief. You are not the architect. You are the household that the Architect is building.
Notice, too, what the psalm does not promise. It does not say children are easy, or that the house will never crack, or that every arrow flies straight. It says they are an heritage — an inheritance to be kept for the next generation — and that the family who knows this will not be ashamed at the gate. Teaching a child that she is treasured is not teaching her that she is perfect. It is teaching her that she was given on purpose, to people who are glad, by a Father who is still building the house around her.
Historical Background
Psalm 127 is one of the fifteen Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120 to 134), the little collection pilgrims sang on the road up to Jerusalem for the feasts. Families walked those roads together — Luke says Jesus was twelve when His family made the trip and did not notice for a day that He was missing — and the ascent songs are full of the things travelling families talk about: home, children, work, sleep, the city gate. Tonight's is the only ascent song with Solomon's name on it, and only one other psalm in the whole book carries his name. Solomon was the king who built the house of the Lord, and the psalm's first line is a builder's confession: except the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it.
The Hebrew has a joke in it that the English cannot keep. The word for build is banah; the word for sons is banim; the word for house is bayit. In Hebrew a house is built out of children — the verb and the noun are the same family — so when verse 3 turns from building to children it has not changed the subject at all. The house the Lord builds in verse 1 is the family of verse 3. The psalm then pictures a father with a quiver of arrows standing in the city gate, which was the courtroom and the marketplace of an ancient town, the place a man's standing was decided. Children were his defence and his witnesses.
The picture tonight's story uses — little ones like young plants round a table — actually comes from the next song on the road, Psalm 128, which is Psalm 127's twin: thy children like olive plants round about thy table. Olive shoots grow up from the roots of the old tree in a ring, and a farmer lets them, because they are the orchard's future. The two psalms were sung one after the other, and the second answers the first: the house the Lord builds turns out to have a table in it, and around the table, in a ring, the children He gave. That is the scene Willow saw at dinner.

"The Family-Centered Gospel of Jesus Christ"
The title is the teaching, and it is tonight's psalm in five words. The gospel of Jesus Christ is family-centered: the house the Lord builds is not an optional extra to the plan of salvation, it is the shape of the plan. A parent reading Psalm 127 beside this talk will find the same two ideas in the same order — that the home is Heavenly Father's building project before it is ours, and that children are His heritage before they are our responsibility. Read it with verse 3 open and a child's name in mind.
